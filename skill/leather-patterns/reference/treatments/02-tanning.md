# Window 2 — tanning

Tanning is the step that makes a hide into leather: it cross-links collagen so the protein no
longer putrefies, no longer stiffens into rawhide, and no longer dissolves on heating.

**Everything else in this folder is reversible or adjustable. The tan is the identity of the
material.** It determines heat resistance, water behaviour, ageing and whether the leather can
ever develop a patina.

---

## The master comparison

| | **Vegetable** | **Chrome (III)** | **Aldehyde / wet-white** | **Synthetic (syntan)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agent | plant polyphenols — bark, wood, leaves, roots (oak, chestnut, mimosa, quebracho, tara, gambier) | basic chromium(III) sulphate | glutaraldehyde, oxazolidine, other aldehydes | sulphonated phenols, acrylates, polyurethanes |
| Bond type | hydrogen bonding + polyphenol cross-links | **coordinate covalent**, each Cr can bond up to six sites ✓ | **covalent** to lysine amino groups ✓ | varies; usually supplementary |
| Time | **30–60 days** (vat tanning can take up to a year) ✓~ | **24–48 hours** ✓ | 24–48 hours ✓ | hours; rarely used alone ✓ |
| **Shrinkage temperature (Ts)** | **70–85 °C** ✓ (75–80 for hydrolysable/pyrogallol tans ✓; up to ~85.8 with Cassia fistula bark ~) | **>100 °C**, typically 100–105 °C ✓ | **>85 °C**; epoxy resin on sheepskin reached 89 °C, ~95 °C achievable with retan ✓ | not a primary tan |
| Hand | **firm, robust, "body"** ✓ | **soft, supple immediately** ✓ | **very soft**; flexural rigidity quoted <2–2.5 N·m ✓~ | — |
| Water resistance | **low** — more hydrophilic sites, more open structure ✓ | **high** — Cr bonds make collagen more hydrophobic ✓ | **poor** (hydrostatic <300 mm ✓~) | — |
| Patina | **yes** — tannins oxidise, leather darkens and softens ✓ | **no** — Cr blocks oil penetration and does not oxidise ✓ | **no**, and it **yellows under UV** ✓~ | — |
| Colour before finish | warm tan / honey | **pale blue-grey — "wet blue"** ✓ | **white — "wet white"** ✓ | — |
| Dye behaviour | accepts aniline dyes readily — tannins **do not occupy all bonding sites** ✓ | **requires different dye chemistry — Cr occupies many sites** ✓ | **more dye and fatliquor needed** for a given depth ✓ | — |
| Market share | small, high-value | **80–90 % of world production** ✓ | growing (chrome-free demand) | supplementary |
| Main liability | slow, less water-resistant, water-spots ✓ | environmental load of Cr effluent; **no patina**; harder to repair | poor water resistance, UV yellowing ✓ | rarely standalone |

**Read the Ts row as the single best proxy for "how tanning-stable is this?"** It is a
standardised measure, so it is one of the few numbers in this folder that can be compared
across sources.

## The less common and historical tans

### Alum / tawing — technically *not* tanning

Alum (potassium aluminium sulphate) plus salt and egg yolk or flour. Historically the
"white leather" of gloves and bookbinding.

- **It is not a true tannage.** Aluminium does not cross-link collagen the way Cr or polyphenols
  do, so the material is not stable to washing and can revert to rawhide if the alum is leached.
- The traditional test: **alum-tawed leather shrinks and stiffens in water.** That is the tell.
- Modern "alum-tanned" products usually have a vegetable or aldehyde component; ask.

### Combination / semi-vegetable

The most common real-world answer, and the one most often mislabelled. A typical route: **light
vegetable tan for body and structure, then a light chrome tan for softness and water
resistance** ✓. The sequence matters — chrome-then-veg and veg-then-chrome give different results.

> **Why it exists:** the tanner wants the vegetable patina and firmness *plus* the chrome
> softness and water resistance. Combination tanning buys both and pays in process control —
> mismanaged, it gives **uneven tanning** ✓.

### Brain tanning

The traditional Native American method: emulsified brain (lecithin) worked into the hide, then
smoked. Chemically an **aldehyde/emulsion tan** in effect. Produces an exceptionally soft,
high-stretch, smoke-scented hide that is **not structural** — do not pattern it as if it were
veg-tan.

### Oil tanning / chamoising

Fish oil oxidising into the fibre (cod oil, historically). Produces **chamois** — very
absorbent, very soft, washable, used for polishing cloths and gloves. The leather that made
"chamois" a colour name.

### Formaldehyde / melamine / oxazolidine

Specialty tans for **white and washable** leathers ✓. Formaldehyde-based tannages are the
historical route to truly washable garment leather.

### Phosphonium compounds (THPS)

A modern chrome-free tan, often used in **combination with vegetable tanning and aluminium**,
where the complex combination achieves a **Ts of 110 °C** — above what veg + Al alone gives ✓.
Also appears in the transparent-leather patent literature as a fixative component.

### Zirconium and titanium

Chrome-free mineral tans used in "wet white" systems and in combination. Not common as sole
tannages; the properties sit between chrome and aldehyde.

## The zirconium-free metal-free comparison — the numbers that exist

A manufacturer comparison of alternative systems, measured by DSC ✓~:

| System | Degradation onset | Enthalpy |
|---|---|---|
| **Chrome sulphate** | **110 °C** | **52 J/g** |
| Aluminium triformate | 68 °C | 22 J/g |
| Zeolite-based | 69 °C | 15 J/g |
| **Tara tannin + zeolite blend** | **Ts 98.6 °C** — beats Cr–Al at 89.1 °C and THPS–LA at 82.6 °C. Tear strength 15.9 N, stretch 42.3 % | — |

**What this tells you:** the metal-free systems are genuinely closing the thermal gap, and
**tannin–zeolite blends are the most promising of them.** But chrome remains materially more
stable, and the enthalpy figures (a measure of how much energy the structure absorbs before
breaking down) show a gap that Ts alone understates.

## What tanning changes, in the pattern's terms

| Decision | Effect | Pattern consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetable | firm, holds shape, patinas, water-spots | good for straps, belts, moulded forms; expect it to darken and soften; **stamp and tool it** |
| Chrome | soft, uniform, water-resistant, won't patina | good for linings, garments, small goods; **do not promise a patina** |
| Aldehyde | softest, white, hypoallergenic, yellowing | good for gloves and medical; **avoid UV exposure claims** |
| Combination | compromise with process risk | ask which order — it changes the result |
| Alum-tawed | not stable to water | do not specify for anything that gets wet |

## Two things this file will not say

1. **That chrome is "toxic".** The industry uses **trivalent chromium, Cr(III)**, which is
   distinct from hexavalent Cr(VI), a carcinogen **not used in tanning** ✓~. Cr(VI) can form as
   a *degradation product* under some conditions and is regulated — that is a real concern, but
   "chrome leather contains chromium(VI)" is a different and often false claim. State the
   distinction or say nothing.
2. **That vegetable tanning is "better".** It is slower, less water-resistant and produces a
   different material. The 80–90 % chrome share is not an accident of marketing.

## Where the numbers come from

Shrinkage temperatures, times, market share and the Cr(III)/Cr(VI) distinction are consistent
across multiple tannery and industry sources ✓. The mechanical figures (tear strength 15.9 N,
flexural rigidity, hydrostatic resistance) are **single-source vendor or vendor-adjacent** and
are marked `~` — directional, not citable.

# Part 2 — Every technique, not just every chemistry

The comparison above answers *what chemistry*. This answers *how it is applied*, and
*which member of each family*. Both change the leather.

## Process routes

| Route | How | Time | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pit tanning** | hides suspended in a series of **increasingly strong liquor pits**, moved by hand over weeks to months | **weeks–months** ✓ | the deepest, most uniform tannin penetration; the classic sole-leather and saddle route |
| **Layaway / rocker** | hides laid flat and rocked through the liquor, then **stacked to "age"** between passes | — | traditional; slow, gentle |
| **Drum tanning** | rotating drum, liquor and hides tumbled | **hours–days** ✓ | fast, uniform, mechanical action softens as it tans; the industrial default for **both** veg and chrome |
| **Yankee / paddle** | paddle wheel in an open vat | — | gentler than a drum |
| **Suspension / "rapid"** | hides hung and liquor pumped through | — | compromise |
| **Combination machine tanning** | drum then pit, or vice versa | — | used where both speed and penetration are wanted |

**Pit vs drum is not nostalgia.** A drum gives speed and mechanical softening; a pit gives
**penetration without mechanical working**. That is why the leathers that must be dense and
uniform through the thickness — sole bends, belt bends, strap butts — are still pit-tanned.

## Chrome — three commercial variants

| Variant | Chrome offer | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-bath** | ~3–5 % Cr₂O₃ by weight ✓ | the modern standard; one float, chromic salt plus a masking/ basifying agent |
| **Two-bath** | chrome applied as dichromate then reduced in the fibre | historic; still used where a particular distribution is wanted |
| **High-exhaustion / low-chrome** | reduced offer with high exhaustion aids | the environmental direction of travel |

**"Chrome 3–5 % by weight"** is the figure to know — it is a small percentage for the effect it
has, which is why chrome displaced everything else so fast.

## Vegetable tannins — which one, and what it does

Veg tanning is not one treatment. The tannin source changes the colour, the hand and the ageing.

| Tannin | Type | Colour | What it is chosen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mimosa (wattle)** | **condensed** | **light pink → brown** ✓ | **versatility and fast penetration with good fullness** ✓ — the modern workhorse. Exposure to air and light **progressively changes the colour**, which is desirable where an aged look is wanted ✓ |
| **Quebracho** | **condensed** | **reddish / rosy / warm yellowish-brown** ✓ | **fullness and roundness** plus a warm colour — "filling and rounding properties" ✓. The vacchetta and burnished-leather tannin ✓. Unsulphited extract is barely soluble and is used for **hot-pit sole tannage**, giving excellent water resistance ✓ |
| **Chestnut** | **hydrolysable** | **dark brown** ✓ | **firmness, compactness, abrasion resistance** ✓ — **the main sole-leather tannin** ✓. Strongly **astringent**, so it penetrates slowly but fixes itself irreversibly and in large amount ✓. Very good light fastness ✓ |
| **Tara** | **hydrolysable (pyrogallic)** | **very light, almost no colouring matter** ✓ | light colour **plus** performance: fullness and softness with a **fine closed grain**, and **the highest grain resistance to breaking load of any vegetable tannin** ✓. Best light and heat fastness of the veg tannins ✓. The automotive and pale-leather tannin |
| **Sumac** | **hydrolysable — the purest** ✓ | **yellow-hazel, almost white** ✓ | the classic **pale** leather; gives "a full and even leather" and **excellent dye levelness with both anionic and basic dyes** ✓. Tannin is **destroyed by boiling** — extracted at 50–60 °C ✓ |
| **Oak bark** | mixed | pale gold → deep brown | the traditional European sole, harness and oak-bark tannage; very slow |
| **Valonia** | hydrolysable | pale green-brown | heavy, dense; sole leather |
| **Gallnut** | hydrolysable | pale | the reference hydrolysable tannin; **the one that pairs with iron for black** |
| **Myrobalan** | hydrolysable | pale | pretanning and pale leathers; widely used in suede |
| **Gambier** | condensed | **low reflectance — darkens the leather** ✓ | soft; traditional in Asia |
| **Cutch / catechu** | condensed | **dark brown-red** | darkening, traditional |
| **Blends** | — | — | **the practical reality.** A tannery uses chestnut to reinforce compactness, another extract to adjust penetration or handle, quebracho for fullness, tara to keep the colour light, mimosa for penetration and filling ✓ |

**Three rules that hold across all of them:**

1. **Condensed vs hydrolysable is the axis that matters.** **Condensed** (mimosa, quebracho,
   cutch) are **catechol-type**: red-brown, astringent, **not decomposed by acids**, and they
   **gradually polymerise into insoluble phlobaphenes** — the reddish sludge called "reds", which
   is why extracts are sulphited (3–8 % sodium bisulphite at 98 °C) to keep them soluble ✓.
   **Hydrolysable** (chestnut, tara, sumac, gallnut, myrobalan, valonia) are **pyrogallol-type**:
   paler, gentler, and **more vulnerable to hydrolysis and acid degradation** ✓.
2. **Light colours need pale tannins, and pale tannins are a smaller set than the market
   assumes.** Tara and sumac are the genuinely pale ones; **vegetable tannins generally have a
   negative effect on light and heat fastness and impart a strong colour to the crust, which makes
   dyeing harder** ✓ — the exact reason a tannery blends a light tannin in.
3. **Veg-tanned leather de-tans and acidifies as it degrades** ✓ (AIC conservation wiki). That is
   why a century-old vegetable-tanned object can be acidic and powdery at the surface, and why
   conservation-grade storage matters.

## Aldehyde — the family, not the one chemical

| Agent | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Glutaraldehyde** | the main modern wet-white tan ✓ |
| **Oxazolidine** | often combined |
| **Formaldehyde** | historic; the genuinely washable leathers |
| **Dialdehyde starch / modified carbohydrates** | the chrome-free, formaldehyde-free direction |
| **Phosphonium (THPS)** | often classed separately; THPS + veg + Al reached **110 °C** ✓~ |

## Other mineral and combination routes

| Route | Ts / note |
|---|---|
| **Aluminium (alum / triformate)** | 68 °C alone ✓~; always used in combination |
| **Zirconium** | wet-white systems, metal but not chrome |
| **Titanium** | niche |
| **Zeolite (silicate)** | 69 °C ✓~; genuinely metal-free |
| **Synthetic/syntan** | supplementary; not a standalone tannage ✓ |
| **Resin tannage** | acrylic/melamine; specialist, gives high Ts and a firm hand |

## The chrome-free claim, decoded

"Chrome-free" means **no chromium in the tanning**. It does not mean:

- metal-free — it may be **aluminium, zirconium or titanium**
- aldehyde-free — glutaraldehyde is a sensitiser and is regulated in some markets
- free of chrome from a **retan** — chrome-tanned then heavily veg-retanned stock is described as
  vegetable-tanned in some markets
- a "clean" process — the alternative chemistries have their own effluent loads

**Ask three questions:** is it chrome-free in the *tan*, in the *retan*, and does it contain
aldehyde? Those are three different promises.

## Specifying a tannage properly

```
TAN   vegetable, pit-tanned, oak bark + chestnut blend — pale→deep brown, firm, will patina
TAN   chrome, single-bath, 4 % — soft, water-resistant, will not patina
TAN   wet-white glutaraldehyde — white, softest, UV-yellows, poor water resistance
TAN   vegetable (tara) + zeolite, metal-free — Ts near 98 °C, firm, pale
TAN   chrome-tanned, vegetable-retanned — chrome substrate plus veg character; NOT chrome-free
```
