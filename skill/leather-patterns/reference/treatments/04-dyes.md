# Dyeing — synthetic classes, natural dyes, mordants and fastness

Dyeing is the most asked-about and least standardised part of finishing. The trade uses a small
number of dye classes with well-understood behaviour, plus a large, poorly-documented natural
dye tradition that is genuinely usable but comes with a hard warning.

**The two questions that matter:** *does the colour sit on the surface or through the leather?*
and *will it stay?*

---

## Part 1 — The industrial dye classes

| Class | How it bonds | Where it is used | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aniline dyes** | soluble, penetrate the fibre — the name comes from the original aniline chemistry and has become a **method descriptor** as much as a chemical one | "aniline leather" = dyed through with soluble dye, minimal or no coating | the most natural look; **lowest stain resistance** ✓ |
| **Acid dyes** | anionic, bond to protonated amino groups at low pH | the workhorse for chrome-tanned leather | the "acid dye" of the leather trade is not the same family as the acid dye of the wool trade, though they behave similarly |
| **Basic dyes** | cationic, bond to carboxyl sites | bright shades, top-dyeing | historically poorer lightfastness; still used for brights |
| **Direct dyes** | substantive, bond by van der Waals / hydrogen bonding without a mordant | through-dyeing | used in transparent-leather work ✓ |
| **Metal-complex dyes** | dye pre-complexed with a metal | deep shades with good fastness | the standard for dark, lightfast colours |
| **Sulphur dyes** | sulphur-containing, reduced then oxidised | blacks and cheap darks | noted as one of the classes used in leather dyeing ✓ |
| **Pigments** (not dyes) | insoluble particles bound in a resin film on the surface | pigmented/semi-aniline finishes | **sits on the leather, does not penetrate** — see `05-finishing.md` |

**Dye penetration is a design decision.** Drum dyeing → colour all the way through, so a cut
edge matches the face. Surface dyeing/spraying → cheaper, but a scuff reveals a pale core. On
**transparent leather**, dye is a special problem: any pigment scatters light and destroys the
effect, which is why transparent leathers are dyed with **direct acid dyes** and even then lose
transmittance ✓.

## Part 2 — Natural dyes

The natural-dye tradition on leather is real, well-documented in craft practice, and **mostly
not industrial**. It works best on **vegetable-tanned leather**, because the tannin matrix gives
the dye something to bond to and because chrome occupies the bonding sites ✓.

### The colour → source table

| Colour | Source | Plant part | Mordant | Fastness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Red** | **madder** (*Rubia rubia tinctorum*) | roots, 3+ years old | **alum** | **excellent** — the standard natural red |
| **Crimson / burgundy** | **cochineal** (*Dactylopius coccus* — an insect) | dried female insects | alum, tin for brighter | good to excellent; leans purple |
| **Raspberry / pink** | **lac** (insect resin) or cochineal | — | alum | good — lac and cochineal "produced a lovely rich raspberry", cochineal the brighter ✓ |
| **Yellow** | **weld** (*Reseda luteola*) | whole plant | alum | **excellent — the most lightfast natural yellow** |
| **Yellow (cheap)** | onion skin, turmeric | — | alum | turmeric is fugitive; onion moderate |
| **Orange-red** | **bloodroot**, madder at higher temperature | root | alum | madder shifts red → **brown above ~70 °C** ✓ |
| **Blue** | **indigo**, **woad** | leaves | **none — vat dye** | **excellent** once oxidised |
| **Purple** | **logwood** (*Haematoxylum campechianum*) | heartwood | alum; iron gives grey | **contested — see the warning below** |
| **Brown** | **walnut hulls** | outer green/black husk | **none — substantive** | **excellent**; the most reliable natural brown ✓ |
| **Brown / tan** | **oak bark**, **quebracho** | bark / wood | alum | good |
| **Black** | **oak galls + iron** ("iron gall"), logwood + iron | — | iron | excellent colour, **at a cost — see below** |
| **Green** | **overdye: yellow then blue** (weld/onion + indigo) ✓ | — | — | good |
| **Purple (mixed)** | overdye: blue then red (madder over indigo) ✓ | — | — | good |

### Recipes, as practised

From craft sources, and worth having because they show the *process*, not just the ingredient ✓~:

**Walnut brown.** Crush black walnut hulls (the outer casing, not the shell); **~1 kg hulls per
2 L water**; boil 2–4 h; strain; apply warm. Multiple coats go golden brown → near-black. No
mordant needed, but it **stains everything it touches** — skin, wood, cloth.

**Madder red.** Grind dried root; soak warm overnight; **simmer, do not boil — above ~70 °C
madder shifts from red toward brown**; strain; apply warm to **alum-mordanted** leather; multiple
coats deepen it.

**Iron-tannin black ("vinegaroon").** Fill a jar with iron scraps, cover with vinegar, seal for
**2–4 weeks** until dark rust-brown. Dye the leather first with a tannin solution (oak bark, tea,
walnut), then apply the iron liquor over it. The black forms instantly by iron–tannin complexing.

**Indigo.** Requires a **reduction vat** — indigo is not soluble until chemically reduced in an
alkaline solution. Dip briefly (1–5 min), remove, expose to air; the colour oxidises from
yellow-green to blue. Repeat for depth.

### Mordants — what they do and why they matter

A **mordant** is a metal salt that bonds dye to fibre and improves fastness ✓. On leather:

| Mordant | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Alum** (potassium aluminium sulphate) | the default. Brightest, truest colours. **~5 g in 100 ml water is adequate; one or two coats** ✓. Also **cleans the leather** and lets it become receptive — tannins leach out into the mordant bath ✓ |
| **Iron** (ferrous sulphate) | **"saddens"** — darkens and shifts toward grey-green and black ✓. The route to black. **Use sparingly — 1–2 tablespoons per litre max; excess iron makes fibre brittle and eventually rots it** ✓ |
| **Tin** | brightens; historically for scarlet. Little used on leather |
| **Copper** | shifts greens and greys |
| **Cream of tartar** | assists alum, particularly for reds |
| **Tannin itself** | acts as a mordant for other dyes, and is the base of iron-gall black |

**Without a mordant, most natural dyes are fugitive.** The reported experience is consistent:
samples without pre-mordanting came out **blotchy and uneven**, and the dye washed out ✓. But
some dyes are **substantive and need no mordant**: **indigo, woad, walnut, lac** ✓.

### The fastness table — and the two warnings

| Dye | Wash fastness | Light fastness | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Madder** | excellent | excellent | the gold standard natural dye ✓ |
| **Indigo / woad** | good–excellent | **excellent** | permanent once oxidised ✓ |
| **Walnut** | excellent | excellent | best natural brown ✓ |
| **Cochineal** | good | good | best natural red, leans purple |
| **Weld** | good | good–excellent | best natural yellow |
| **Logwood** | good | **contested** | ↓ |
| **Most other plant dyes** | poor | poor | "generally not wash-fast or sun-fast" ✓ |

> **Warning 1 — logwood is disputed, and the dispute is instructive.** Sources split into two
> camps: one calls logwood "good colourfastness, moderate lightfastness, improved by iron in the
> bath" ~; the other documents **logwood being outlawed in England and named in Federal uniform
> regulations in the mid-19th century because it faded so fast**, with a first-hand report of
> logwood-dyed cloth going from dark grey to light tan **within 14 days of repeated sunlight** ✓.
> **Do not promise logwood fastness.** Treat it as a beautiful and unreliable dye, and test.

> **Warning 2 — iron black destroys leather slowly.** Iron–tannin black is the traditional route
> to a true black and it works. It also **accelerates degradation over years** ✓. The craft
> guidance is unusually honest about the trade-off: acceptable for an item with a **5–10 year**
> expected life, use sparingly for anything that must last decades ✓.

### Where natural dyeing changes the material, not just the colour

- **Alum mordanting is a treatment, not an inert step.** It alters the leather's receptivity and
  pulls tannins out of it.
- **Iron makes it brittle.** A black that will crack at a fold is a real outcome.
- **Dyeing over a vegetable tan darkens the base** — the tannins themselves oxidise and shift from
  tan toward brown as they age ✓, so a madder red on veg-tan will not stay the colour it was
  applied.
- **Natural dyeing is uneven by nature.** This is a property, not a defect: it is the reason
  hand-dyed leather reads as handmade.

### What does not work

- **Synthetic leather cannot be natural-dyed** — the fibres do not bond with natural dyes ✓.
- **Berries and beets are stains, not dyes** — they fade to brown ✓.
- **Chrome-tanned leather resists natural dyes** because chromium occupies the bonding sites ✓.
  Natural dyeing is a **vegetable-tan process** in practice.

## Part 3 — What to specify

```
COLOUR  drum-dyed through (acid dye), matched across all panels
COLOUR  surface-dyed + waxed, expect the core to be pale at a cut edge
COLOUR  natural: madder on alum-mordanted veg-tan, expect variation and patina shift
COLOUR  natural: walnut, unmordanted, substantive
AVOID   iron-gall black on any part that will flex repeatedly or must last 20+ years
```

## Honest limits

- The industrial dye classes and their behaviour are consistent across sources ✓, but **recipes
  and percentages are tannery-specific** and not public.
- The natural-dye recipes are **craft practice**, not standards. They are reproducible in
  direction and not in exact shade.
- **Fastness ratings are qualitative** — "excellent", "good" — from craft sources ~. They are
  consistent enough to rank dyes, not to certify one.
- The logwood dispute is recorded rather than resolved, because both sets of sources are
  confident and they cannot both be right.

# Part 4 — Application methods

Chemistry decides what the dye *can* do. Application decides **where it goes**, and that is
what a designer specifies.

## The methods

| Method | How | Penetration | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drum dyeing** | dye added to the rotating drum after tanning | **through** ✓ | uniform colour all the way through; **a cut edge matches the face**; the benchmark |
| **Paddle / vat dyeing** | in an open vessel with agitation | through, slower | gentler on soft leathers |
| **Padding** | dye pressed into the surface by roller | partial | controlled depth |
| **Spray dyeing** | sprayed onto the grain | **surface only** | cheap; **a scuff reveals a pale core** |
| **Hand / brush dyeing** | applied by hand | variable | deliberate variation; the craft look |
| **Dip dyeing** | the part is dipped, usually partly | gradient | **ombre / dip-dye edges** — a deliberate gradient |
| **Tie / resist dyeing** | physical masking before dyeing | masked | the resist is a pattern tool |
| **Top dyeing / "tipping"** | a second colour sprayed over a first in the drum | surface highlights | depth and two-tone |
| **Piece dyeing after assembly** | the *finished object* is dyed | through | consistent object colour, harder on components |
| **Screen / block printing** | pigment or dye printed on | surface | pattern; wears at flex |

## The consequences that matter for a pattern

**1 · Drum-dyed → the cut edge is the same colour.** Surface-dyed → **it is not.** That single
fact decides whether a raw-edge design works. A raw-edge wallet needs drum dyeing, or an edge
paint, or it needs to *own* the pale core as a design feature.

**2 · Colour matching across panels is a penetration question.** Two panels from different
dye lots, surface-dyed, will not match under different light. Drum dyeing narrows the gap.

**3 · Ombre and dip-dye are directional.** They must be laid out in the same orientation on the
hide as the pattern intends — an ombre panel cut in the other direction runs the wrong way.
This is a **lay plan constraint**, not a colour choice.

**4 · Resist and masking put the pattern in the dye, not the cut.** The pattern is information
(as with photochromic, `07-avant-garde.md`): a different resist gives a different design from
the same hide. The corollary is that **the pattern cannot be changed without re-dyeing.**

**5 · Dark colours hide grain defects; pale colours expose them.** A pale aniline is the most
honest leather there is and the least forgiving of the hide. If a design wants pale and
uncoated, it is specifying the top grade of hide whether it says so or not.

## Specifying dye application

```
COLOUR  drum-dyed black, through — cut edges match, raw edges viable
COLOUR  surface-dyed + waxed, expect pale core at cut edge — edges must be painted or folded
COLOUR  dip-dye ombre, 40 % depth — all panels laid the same direction on the hide
COLOUR  hand-dyed, variation intended and accepted
COLOUR  dark drum dye is the most defect-tolerant choice
```
