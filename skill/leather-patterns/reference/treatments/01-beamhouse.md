# Window 1 — the beamhouse

The preparation stages. **This is where the leather's ceiling is set.** Beamhouse decisions
determine how stretchy, how soft and how tight-grained a hide *can* become; the tanner can
refine afterwards within that envelope but cannot undo it.

A beamhouse that is over-limed gives a soft, loose, stretchy leather no matter what follows.

---

## The sequence, with what each step actually does

| # | Step | What it does | Effect on the final leather |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Curing / preservation** | salt, chilling or drying the raw hide at the abattoir to stop putrefaction | a badly cured hide is a defect that no later step repairs |
| 2 | **Soaking** | rehydrates the preserved hide, removes salt, dirt, blood, curing agents | incomplete soaking → uneven tanning; also where **bacterial damage** begins if the soak is too long |
| 3 | **Fleshing** | mechanically removes fat and residual tissue from the flesh side | removes the hypodermis; leaves a consistent thickness to work with |
| 4 | **Unhairing / liming** | lime + sodium sulphide dissolves the hair and epidermis, and **swells the hide** | **the single most consequential step for softness and stretch** ✓ |
| 5 | **Splitting** | band-knife splits the hide into grain and flesh layers | sets the layer (L3) you are buying; the flesh split carries the thickness variation ✓ |
| 6 | **Deliming** | removes the lime and **reduces the swelling / turgidity** ✓ | brings the hide back to a workable pH |
| 7 | **Bating** | **enzymes** — proteolytic, acting on non-structural proteins | **"when the goal is to produce a soft leather, this operation is carried out using enzymes"** ✓. Over-bating → loose, empty grain |
| 8 | **Degreasing** | removes natural fat, essential on fat-rich skins | **sheepskin requires it** ✓; residual fat causes greasy spots and uneven dye |
| 9 | **Pickling** | acid + salt lowers pH and prepares the collagen to accept tanning agents | the state called "pelt" — the raw material for both tanning **and transparent leather** |

**Two terms to keep straight:**
- **Pelt / pelts** = the unhaired, limed, delimed, pickled hide — before tanning.
- **Wet blue** = chrome-tanned, still wet. **Wet white** = aldehyde-tanned, still wet.

## The three levers in the beamhouse, and what they cost

### 1. Liming — the swelling lever

Lime swells the hide by opening the fibre structure. **More swelling → softer, stretchier, more
open leather.** Less swelling → firmer, tighter, less stretch.

This is why a **sole bend and a garment lambskin can start as the same chemistry and end as
opposite materials.** Sole leather is limed lightly and kept dense; garment leather is limed
hard and bated long.

### 2. Bating — the softness lever

Enzymes digest the non-structural proteins that cement fibres together. **More bating → softer,
looser grain.** Bating is the step where a tannery decides whether the finished article will
have a tight grain that burnishes, or a loose one that wrinkles.

> **The trade-off:** over-bating produces the defect called **"empty" or "loose" grain** — a
> surface that looks fine flat and then bags and wrinkles the first time it is handled. There is
> no finishing fix for it.

### 3. Splitting — the thickness lever

Splitting **decouples thickness from anatomy** ✓. This is the mechanism behind a point made in
`../anatomy/properties.md`: a split leather can be near-uniform in millimetres while remaining
utterly non-uniform in weave and stretch, because splitting only removes material from the
bottom — it does not change the fibre architecture above it.

---

## What the beamhouse decides, in one table

| Outcome wanted | Beamhouse route | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Firm, dense, toolable** (sole, belt, strap) | light lime, minimal bate, no over-soak | less softness; needs long tanning to penetrate |
| **Soft, drapey, garment** | heavy lime, long bate, degrease well | looser grain, more stretch, lower shape retention |
| **Tight grain for a visible surface** | controlled lime, restrained bate, careful unhairing | less softness |
| **Maximum area** | full swelling, careful splitting | thinner, weaker per unit area |
| **Transparent leather** | full beamhouse, then stop at **pelt** and do not tan ✓ | see `07-avant-garde.md` — a completely different route from here |

## The beamhouse's own failure modes

| Defect | Cause | Whether it can be fixed later |
|---|---|---|
| **Loose / empty grain** | over-bating, over-liming | **no** |
| **Bacterial damage / putrefaction** | slow curing, long soaks, warm water | no — and it is the most common post-slaughter defect ✓ |
| **Uneven tanning** | incomplete soaking or degreasing | partly, via retanning |
| **Greasy spots, uneven dye** | residual fat, especially on sheep | no — it shows through every finish |
| **Butcher cuts, flay marks** | hide removal at the abattoir | no — these are graded, not repaired |
| **Hair root remnants / "scud"** | incomplete unhairing | no |

**That right-hand column is the point of this file.** Every other window can be adjusted after
the fact. The beamhouse cannot. A maker who is choosing leather is, whether they know it or not,
choosing a beamhouse.

## What to ask a supplier

You will rarely get process detail, but three questions are answerable and diagnostic:

1. **"Is this a grain split or a full-thickness hide?"** — tells you the splitting decision.
2. **"How is it bated / how soft is the temper?"** — the softness envelope.
3. **"Any loose grain or empty areas?"** — the one beamhouse defect that survives everything.

## Where the evidence is thin

- **Beamhouse parameters are not standardised.** Times, temperatures and chemical offers are
  tannery-specific and largely trade-secret. The *directions* in this file are reliable; the
  *numbers* are not generally published and are not given here.
- **"Heavy lime → softer" is a general principle** stated consistently across tannery process
  descriptions, but the quantitative relationship is not in the open literature.
- The step list and the role of bating in softness are from tannery process documentation ✓
  (UNIC, Cortina, Leather Naturally).
- **The alternative-unhairing numbers are single-study** ~ (one stock, one tannery, four routes).
  The direction — oxidative weakest, hair-saving strongest — is consistent with the mechanism,
  but the percentages should not be carried to another hide without re-testing.
- **Enzymatic unhairing is not automatically gentler in every respect.** One enzyme study found
  the physical-mechanical properties **not impaired** but the **grain more susceptible to the
  buffing effect and dye absorption less even** ✓. "Green" describes the effluent, not the leather.
- **No enzymatic unhairing technology is reported that causes zero hide damage** — a review
  states this plainly ✓. The claim to be sceptical of is "enzyme unhairing, no trade-off".

# The second axis: *which route*, not just *which step*

The step table above gives one canonical route. **Real tanneries choose between competing
methods at almost every step, and the choice produces a different leather.** This section is
the part the first pass of this file was missing.

## Unhairing — six routes, six different leathers

| Route | Method | Effect on grain | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hair-destroying — sulphide** | lime + sodium sulphide dissolves the hair and epidermis | controlled; the industry default | the bulk of production. **Hair removal generates ~60–70 % of the total pollution load of leather processing** ✓ |
| **Hair-destroying — oxidative ("hair burn")** | peroxide + lime, or chlorinated lime/chlorine dioxide, oxidises the keratin | **measurably weaker** — see the table below ✓ | where sulphide is restricted; cheaper. **Reduces BOD/COD by ~40 %** ✓ |
| **Hair-saving — paint/paste** | thickened sulphide + lime (roughly **50 parts lime : 50 water : 5–20 sulphide**) **painted on the flesh side only** ✓; the hair loosens intact and is recovered | **grain untouched** ✓ — described as producing a **cleaner grain surface** ✓ | **wool recovery on sheep; any hair-on route** |
| **Sweating / putrefaction** | controlled bacterial action in a dark humid chamber, **22–27 °C**, until the hair root loosens and the wool can be pulled ✓ | **historically the finest grain** ✓ — and completely uncontrollable | traditional and historic work; used where **the wool is worth more than the skin** ✓ |
| **Enzymatic (keratinase / protease)** | sulphide-free; hydrolyses keratin | **gentler**, better smoothness and strength; **but "the buffing effect on the grain" is more noticeable and dye absorption less even** ✓ | the "green" route; growing |
| **Mechanical / shearing** | the wool is cut, not the follicle loosened | n/a | sheep, where wool is the product |

### The measured comparison — the one place these routes have numbers

A study comparing four unhairing routes on the same stock, crust leather ✓~:

| Unhairing | Tensile (MPa) | Tear (N) | Elongation % | Ball burst % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conventional (sulphide)** | 30.3 ± 17.8 | 40.1 ± 9.6 | 60.5 ± 28.5 | 12.6 |
| **Hair-saving** | **32.1 ± 21.5** | **46.4 ± 11.3** | **64.2 ± 22.9** | **13.3** |
| **Painting** | 30.3 ± 8.1 | 38.6 ± 9.4 | 41.5 ± 17.1 | 11.0 |
| **Oxidative** | **21.8 ± 6.5** | **34.1 ± 12.1** | **39.6 ± 10.1** | **9.6** |

**Read the oxidative row.** It is **~28 % weaker in tensile, ~15 % weaker in tear, and ~35 % lower
in elongation** than the conventional route, and it **failed the grain-crack standard** the study
was testing against ✓. The proposed mechanism is a larger pH shift changing the isoelectric point
and more collagen side-chain breaking.

**This is the single most useful number in the file:** *the cheapest, most "environmentally
friendly-sounding" alternative to sulphide unhairing produces a measurably weaker leather.*
Environmentally better and mechanically worse are not opposites — they are a trade-off, and this
one is measured.

> **The load-bearing consequence:** **hair-saving and hair-destroying are not variants of one
> process, they are two different products.** If the hair is to stay on the leather, it must
> never be destroyed — which rules out the entire sulphide route and the whole downstream
> chemistry that goes with it. See `09-hair-on-shearling.md`.

## Preservation — before the beamhouse

| Method | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Salt curing (wet-salted)** | the default: ~40–50 % sodium chloride, stacked ✓~. Creates the chloride load that makes tannery effluent hard to treat |
| **Brine curing** | immersion in saturated brine; more uniform, more controllable |
| **Chilling / cold chain** | no salt at all; requires the tannery to be near the abattoir |
| **Drying** | air/sun drying; used where salt is expensive |
| **"Fresh hide to wet-blue"** | **no curing step at all** — the hide goes into the process within hours ✓. Removes salt from the effluent entirely, which is the single biggest environmental win available in the process. This is where the industry is moving |
| **Biocide treatment** | added to the soak to suppress bacteria |

**Why a designer should care:** a hide that was badly preserved is a hide with bacterial damage,
and bacterial damage is **invisible under a finish and fatal to strength**. `01` on the anatomy
side already says this; here is where it happens.

## Bating agents — including the ones nobody puts on a label

| Agent | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Pancreatic enzymes (trypsin)** | the classic; still the reference |
| **Bacterial / fungal proteases** | modern, more controllable, more specific |
| **"Pure" — dog dung** | the historical bating agent, used for centuries; the enzymes are the same class ✓ |
| **Bird guano** | the other historical bating agent |
| **Urine / ammonia** | historical deliming and bating |

> **The history is not decoration.** The reason bating is the softness step is that people
> discovered, empirically, that a hide soaked in dung came out softer. The enzyme mechanism was
> worked out afterwards. That is a good reminder that the process is older than its explanation.

## Degreasing — three routes

| Route | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Aqueous (surfactant)** | the modern default; emulsifies the fat into the float |
| **Solvent** | perchloroethylene historically; effective on very fatty skins, being phased out ✓ |
| **Enzymatic (lipases)** | targeted, milder, growing |
| **Mechanical pressure degreasing** | squeezing fat out on the fleshing machine before chemistry |

**Essential on pig and sheep.** Pig has a full-depth follicle structure (`../anatomy/species/porcine.md`);
sheep carries a lot of natural grease, and residual fat ruins dye levelness and shows through
every finish.

## Pickling — and the move away from it

| Route | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Conventional pickle** | salt + sulphuric/formic acid to pH ~2.5–3 ✓; the pelt is preserved and acidified ready for chrome |
| **Low-salt / salt-free** | reduces the chloride load |
| **Pickle-free chrome** | chrome tanning applied directly after bating, at higher pH, with masking agents ✓ — removes a whole step and a salt load |
| **Solvent / non-aqueous** | niche |

**The pickle is also the gateway to the non-tanning routes.** Transparent leather starts at the
pelt (`07-avant-garde.md`); so does any process that wants to modify collagen before it is
cross-linked.
