# The horse — the most structured hide in the trade

Horse is the best case study for this whole folder, because the equine trade did not
settle for region names. It identifies a **layer**, and that layer is worth more than
everything around it. If you understand why shell cordovan exists, you understand the
difference between L1/L2 and L3 in one example.

---

## 1. The primary division: front and butt

The standard cut ✓: after removal, the hide is **measured 18 inches (460 mm) forward
from the root of the tail along the backbone**, and cut **at right angles to the
backbone**. That gives two pieces:

| Piece | What it is | Thickness | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **front** — a.k.a. **front quarter horsehide (FQHH)**, sold as **single horsefront (SHF)** or **double horsefront (DHF)** | the forepart; about **two-thirds of the hide area** ✓ | thin — e.g. 2.5–3 oz / **1.0–1.2 mm** for Horween's DHF ✓ | jackets, gloves, shoe tops, baseballs |
| **butt** | the rear part, everything behind the 460 mm line | thick, **4–10 oz / 1.6–4 mm** for the strip ✓ | strip, shell, holsters, sheaths |

**Why the split is where it is:** the 460 mm line is the practical boundary of the
hyaline layer. Behind it, the tissue is different. Ahead of it, it is ordinary hide.

## 2. What the butt actually contains — four things, nested

Working outward from the middle of the rear:

```
        ┌──────────────────────────────┐
        │  BUTT  (L2 cut, the whole rear)│
        │  ┌────────────────────────┐  │
        │  │ STRIP  (band in front   │  │   <- a.k.a. horsebutt, bark strip,
        │  │ of the shells, 1.6-4 mm)│  │      "north of the cordovan cut"
        │  └────────────────────────┘  │
        │  ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐  │
        │  │  SHELL   │  │  SHELL   │  │   <- hyaline layer, L3, one per side
        │  │ (oval)   │  │ (oval)   │  │      ~1-2.5 sq ft each
        │  └──────────┘  └──────────┘  │
        └──────────────────────────────┘
```

| Term | Level | What it is | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **butt** | L2 | the whole rear section | ✓ |
| **strip** / **horsebutt strip** / **bark strip** / **russet horsehide strip** / **"north of cordovan"** | L2 | the band **just in front of** the shells, extending from the hip down to each cheek. Thick, coarse pronounced grain | ✓ |
| **shell** / **shell cordovan** | L3 | the dense hyaline membrane, one **oval panel each side** of the rump, roughly the size of a book or small platter | ✓ |
| **culatta** | L2 (equine usage) | **butt leather with shell traces on the flesh side** — a full-grain horse leather with patches of shell coating the reverse | ✓ |

### The "horse butt" naming trap

Multiple sources note that **"horse butt" is a misnomer** for what is sold as horsebutt
strips: **the strips are cut from *in front of* the butt section** ✓. The strip is the
band above/forward of the shells, not the butt itself. Tanners and retailers use "horse
butt" loosely; the same leather is also called a **strip**.

> **For a spec:** write **"equine strip (horsebutt), 1.6–2.0 mm, above the shell"** and
> not "horse butt". If a supplier says "horse butt" ask whether they mean strip, shell,
> or the whole rear cut. Three different materials, one phrase.

## 3. Shell cordovan — the anatomy, and why it behaves as it does

**What it is.** A **hyaline layer** — described in the leather literature as a
"collagen protein based tissue structure that is a cross between flesh and cartilage" —
lying **between the epidermis and the corium**, and **present only on the rump of the
horse**. It does not exist in bovine skin ✓.

Careful: several accessible sources describe the shell as a "flat muscle", "subcutaneous
fascia", or the **panniculus carnosus**. The panniculus carnosus attribution is explicitly
**retracted by the writer who proposed it** ✓, and the confusion is explained by the
filament morphology: shell filaments taper and fold over on themselves, resembling actin
(muscle) filaments in scale, which is why the muscle story keeps circulating. **Treat
"shell = muscle" as wrong, and "shell = hyaline layer between epidermis and corium" as
the defensible description.**

**Why it does not crease.** Filament scale, from the same account ✓:

| | filament diameter | arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| ordinary skin collagen | ~80 nm | discrete fibrils |
| shell hyaline layer | ~12 nm bulbous head, tapering | folded over on themselves, roughly **ten filaments per one** of ordinary hide |

That packing is the whole story. It has **no real grain side** — no follicle pattern,
no pores — so there is nothing to break into a crease. Under bending it **rolls**
instead of creasing. It also **retains oils and waxes far better**, which is why
cordovan is famously low-maintenance and the "condition it often" advice does not apply.

**Consequences for a pattern:**

| Property | Value | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Thickness | ~1.5 mm finished (traditional); not oz-rated like a split | different strain screen from a 1 mm hide |
| Grain | none | no grain-crack failure mode at all; the `durability.md` §2b film-substrate wrinkle model **does not apply** |
| Stretch | very low | can be used for structural parts; do not add a lining to stop stretch |
| Creasing | rolls | a hinge in cordovan will not craze; it will develop soft ripples |
| Yield | two ovals per horse, **~1–2.5 sq ft each** | you cannot get a large panel. Design *around* an oval |
| Cost driver | two shells per animal + **6–12 months** of veg-tanning and hand finishing ✓ | a shell is not a cheaper butt; it is a different product |

**The pattern-design point.** Shell cordovan is the one leather where the **shape of the
raw material constrains the design**, not just its area. An oval of ~1.5 sq ft dictates
short, wide parts: watch straps, card wallets, small flaps, the quarters of a shoe. If a
design needs a 300 mm continuous run, cordovan is the wrong material regardless of
budget. This is the flat-goods analogue of "you cannot flatten a doubly-curved surface".

## 4. Culatta — the equine-only reading

In the equine trade, **culatta** is a *composite* material: the butt leather **plus the
shell traces that remain on the flesh side** where the shells were removed ✓.

Sold in three forms ✓:

| Form | What you get |
|---|---|
| **horse culatta** | full-grain leather with some shell coating on the flesh side |
| **split horse culatta** | a split (corium) leather with areas of shell coating on the flesh side |
| **shell cordovan** | the shell alone — no corium, no grain |

**The layered anatomy**, from the grain down ✓: full grain → grain–corium connection →
suede/split/corium (anatomically the reticular layer) → **culatta layer** (a separating
layer that exists **only above the shell**) → **shell**.

Three practical consequences:

1. **The same piece changes character across its face.** Some areas feel fluffy (suede/
   corium), others feel coated (shell traces). That is not a defect; it is the material.
2. **Cutting through the culatta layer deliberately reveals a wrinkly pattern** ✓ — a
   unique decorative effect, and a reminder that a "defect" is often just an exposed
   layer.
3. **The white residue in the wrinkles is normal tannery condition**, not bloom to be
   cleaned off ✓. It dulls the shine and settles with age.

> **Spec warning:** the *bovine* culatta (butt + belly middles + hind shanks) and the
> *equine* culatta (butt with shell traces) are unrelated materials sharing a word. See
> `taxonomy.md`. Species must be stated.

## 5. Horse vs cow, as a material choice

From Horween's own comparison ✓, which is refreshingly unromantic:

| | horsehide | cow/steerhide |
|---|---|---|
| Grain | **coarser, more pronounced** | finer, still pronounced |
| Weight consistency | **big variation even within one hide**; generally lighter/thinner | **much more consistent**, better yields |
| Fibre structure | different | — |
| Natural markings | **much more variation** | fewer |
| Abrasion | more resistant | — |
| Supply | scarcer | readily available |
| Sold as | **three separate products**: front, strip, shells | **sides** |

**That second row is the one that matters for pattern work.** Cowhide is more
*consistent in weight and usable area*, which is exactly the property a repeatable
cutting layout needs. Horsehide's variation is a feature for a maker designing one
object around a beautiful piece, and a liability for anything that must nest reliably.

Note also: horsehide is generally a **by-product of the meat trade** in the countries
where horses are slaughtered for food ✓ — it is not farmed for leather.

## 6. Other equine terms

- **Cheek** — the rounded area low on each side of the butt, below/beside the shell.
  Part of the strip's extent ("from the hip down to each cheek") ✓.
- **Hind shank / leg** — dense but small.
- **Shell grading** — tanneries grade shells (Grade 1, 2, X, etc.) on cleanliness and
  uniformity ✓. A Grade 2 shell is not weaker; it has more surface character.
- **"Cordovan" as a colour** is not the material. Both are real; do not confuse them.

## 7. Summary table for a cutting plan

| Material | Thickness | Grain | Stretch | Best for | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| horse **front** (FQHH/DHF) | 1.0–1.2 mm | coarse, marked | low | thin flexible panels, garment-weight | high-wear edges; too thin for structure |
| horse **strip** (horsebutt) | 1.6–4 mm | very pronounced coarse | very low | belts, straps, holsters, sheaths, boot uppers | fine small goods where thickness fights you |
| horse **culatta** | varies across the piece | mixed: grain + suede + shell | mixed | artisanal footwear, pieces where the texture change is the design | anything needing uniform surface |
| **shell cordovan** | ~1.5 mm | none | lowest | watch straps, card wallets, small flaps, shoe quarters | any large continuous panel |
