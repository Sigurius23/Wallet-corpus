# Kangaroo, deer, and the remaining species

The species where **the whole skin behaves like one region** — and therefore the species where
this folder's regional taxonomy buys you the least and its structural facts buy you the most.

---

## Kangaroo

### Why it is structurally different

| Property | Kangaroo | Cowhide |
|---|---|---|
| Collagen fibre | **fine, concentrated, highly uniform, running parallel to the surface** ✓ | irregular, bundled |
| Angle of weave | **low** ✓ | higher, varying by region |
| Grain layer | **very thin** ✓ | thicker |
| Sweat glands / erector pili muscles | **absent** ✓ | present |
| Fat content | **low** — a "dry" leather ✓ | higher |
| Elastin | **evenly distributed throughout** ✓ | concentrated near the surface |
| Thickness | naturally **0.8–1.0 mm** (≈2 oz) ✓ | 1.2–1.4 mm |

That combination — uniform parallel fibre, low weave angle, no glands to leave voids, thin
grain — is why **no splits or heavy shaving are needed** ✓, and why the leather is thin and
strong at the same time.

**The single most useful fact:** because the fibres lie flat and parallel, **splitting cuts
fewer of them**. A thick hide loses most of its strength when skived; kangaroo does not.
This is the mechanism behind all the strength claims, and it is the one worth repeating.

### What is improved by it

- **Consistency across the hide and between hides is remarkable** ✓ — quoted by users as the
  reason it is chosen for competition archery tabs. For a pattern maker, **this is the
  headline: kangaroo is the most predictable leather in the folder.** Regional guidance
  matters least here.
- Thin and abrasion-resistant → a good candidate for **thin flexible parts** and for linings
  in small goods.

### What is not

- **It creases easily** — though the creasing usually does not lead to cracking, because the
  fibres are strong ✓
- **Not the smoothest** unless corrected, and **almost all kangaroo has scars** ✓ — which one
  supplier states plainly are not to be treated as waste areas ~
- Matte texture, patinas and darkens quickly ✓

### The strength claims — handle with care

Vendors quote: **"10× the tensile strength of cowhide"** ✓~, **"cowhide of the same thickness
is roughly 3× weaker"** ✓~, and a skiving comparison — **kangaroo skived to 20 % of its
thickness retains 30–60 % of its tensile strength, versus calf at 1–4 %** ✓~.

These come from **commercial and enthusiast sources with no test method attached**, and they
disagree with each other by a factor of three. The **mechanism** (parallel fibres, low weave
angle, fewer fibres severed by skiving) is solid and is stated consistently. **Quote the
mechanism, not the multiple.**

## Deer, elk, moose

- **Deerskin** — soft, supple, famously quiet and tough for its weight; thin grain; used for
  gloves, garments, and as a lining/backing. Very high extension.
- **Elk / moose** — heavier, thicker fibre, very abrasion-resistant; sold as whole hides for
  moccasins, bags, and work gloves.
- **Braintanned / smoke-tanned deer** is a different material again — a soft, high-stretch,
  non-structural hide. Do not pattern it as if it were veg-tan.

**Region guidance:** these are sold as whole hides or halves and the literature on regional
variation within a deer skin is thin. Use the general rule — back and shoulder firmer, belly
and neck looser — and **check the actual hide**, because the species-level data does not
exist to be more precise than that. Saying so is more honest than inventing a map.

## Camel

- **Extremely high fibre density** — commonly quoted at **~10× the fibres per unit area of
  cowhide** ✓~ — and correspondingly rugged
- **Stiff** with a hard finish that resists scuffing; may **lose colour under hard wear** and
  needs frequent conditioning ✓
- Scarce: much of the supply is from animals that died naturally ✓
- Thick and stable → shoes, heavy bags, belts, sandals, wallets ✓

**Pattern note:** camel is a **structural** leather, not a soft one. Treat it like thick
buffalo rather than like a large calf.

## Buffalo (water buffalo)

Covered in `bovine.md` but worth repeating because it is often sold as a budget exotic:
**thicker fibres, more widely spaced, pronounced pebbly grain, a sub-dermal protective layer,
and less flexible than cowhide at equal substance** ✓~. High breathability and temperature
tolerance ✓~. Excellent for straps and heavy goods; poor for anything needing drape.

---

## Seal, walrus, and the rest

Named in the general leather literature ✓~ and tanned historically (Inuit fish-skin and seal
traditions ✓). Modern supply is heavily restricted — **marine mammal products are regulated
in most jurisdictions and the rules are not a leather question.** If a design specifies them,
the constraint is legal, not anatomical, and it should be raised as such.

## The rule this file exists to make

**Species with uniform fibre structure make the regional taxonomy unnecessary.** Kangaroo is
the clearest case: the whole skin is one region. That is *not* a reason to skip this folder —
it is a reason to check **which kind of leather you have before applying regional rules**:

| If the hide is… | Then… |
|---|---|
| Regionally structured (bovine, equine, ovine, caprine) | cut by region; the map matters |
| Uniform by construction (kangaroo) | the map does not matter; consistency and thickness do |
| Plated or armoured (crocodilian, stingray, ostrich leg) | the hard-part level dominates everything — see `../appendages.md` |
| A narrow tube or strip (snake, salmon, ostrich leg) | **shape** dominates; design around the outline, not the regions |
