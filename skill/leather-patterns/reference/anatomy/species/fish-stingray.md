# Fish — stingray, shark, and the scaled food-fish skins

Fish leather inverts a rule that holds for every land animal in this folder: **the fibre
structure runs crosswise rather than parallel to the spine** ✓. That single fact makes fish
skins stronger per unit thickness than most leathers and, in the stingray, produces the
hardest surface in common use.

---

## Stingray — the armour

### The anatomy

**Dorsal skin is calcium-rich with bony protrusions — denticles — larger in the centre of the
skin and smaller toward the outer edges** ✓.

Each denticle is a three-layer structure: **a hard enameloid shell, a dentin core, and a pulp
cavity** ✓. The enameloid is **fluorapatite** — calcium phosphate with fluoride in the crystal,
about 3 % by weight — not the hydroxyapatite of tooth enamel ✓.

**The critical growth fact: stingray denticles cannot grow larger once formed.** As the animal
grows it adds **new** denticles between existing ones, each fixed in size from the moment it
develops ✓. That is exactly why the bead pattern varies across the hide and why a hide is
described as having larger beads centrally and smaller at the edges.

### The "crown" or "eye"

Every genuine stingray hide has a **cluster of larger, more pronounced denticles near the
centre** ✓ — called the **crown** or **eye**. Anatomically these are **tubercles running from
the nuchal region behind the head down the midline of the disc**, and they are typically
**larger and more densely packed in females** ✓.

This is a **layout feature, not a defect**: it is the most characterful part of the skin and
it has a fixed position. A large flat panel will contain it whether you want it or not.

### The properties

| Property | Value | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Surface hardness | enameloid caps, around **Mohs 5** — a key slides off ✓ | highest scratch resistance of any leather in common use |
| Peel behaviour | beads are **fused to the hide** — nothing peels, flakes or lifts ✓ | the opposite of snake. No delamination failure mode |
| Flexibility | **low — the panel wants to stay flat** ✓ | accessory, panel and inlay work only. Never a gusset, never a hinge |
| Hide size | small | wallets, straps, cases, inlays, cuffs |
| Yield | only about **a quarter of the ray's skin is tanned**; the wings supply the rest as food ~ | plan for small pieces |
| Care | dry cloth; **no conditioner** ✓ | conditioning the matrix, not the beads |

### Pattern consequences

1. **One flat plane.** Design as for a rigid sheet: book-cover, inlay, panel, belt facing.
   If the part must bend, the beads resist it and the inter-bead matrix takes all the strain.
2. **The crown is fixed in the middle.** On a wallet the crown becomes the visual centre —
   design around it or for it.
3. **Edges are the weak point.** On stingray the parts needing attention are **the edges, the
   interior lining, and the fold** ✓ — because the beads run right out to the edge and cannot
   be turned.
4. **Stitch lines cross beads.** You are stitching through a composite. Where a seam must go,
   keep it in the inter-bead valleys, and expect the needle to deflect off a bead crown.

## Shark — the same word, sanded

**Shark and stingray were both called shagreen for centuries** ✓. The difference is treatment:
shark denticles are **small, sharp, arranged in regular rows toward the tail**, and are
**usually ground off** during tanning, leaving a fine pebbled grain ✓.

| | stingray | shark |
|---|---|---|
| Denticles | large, rounded, irregular; biggest along the spine | small, sharp, regular rows toward the tail |
| After tanning | **kept**, polished flat into a bead mosaic | **usually ground off** |
| Feel | hard, pearlescent | conventional leather |
| Flexibility | low | high — tiny scales let the hide bend |
| Size | small — accessories and panels | large, roughly **30 × 20 in** at the widest |
| Care | dry cloth, never conditioner | conditions like fine leather, softens with age |

**So shark is a normal leather and stingray is armour.** Do not specify "shagreen".

## The scaled food-fish skins — salmon, tilapia, pirarucu/arapaima, carp

Small skins, strong per thickness, and — importantly — **region matters, but not in a
consistent direction.**

From a three-species study (*Oreochromis niloticus*, *Arapaima gigas*, *Cynoscion virescens*)
✓:

| Species | Region effect | Direction effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Nile tilapia** | tensile **not** significantly different by region | **longitudinal > diagonal** in tensile; tears more easily at head and tail |
| **Pirarucu / paiche** | head region **weaker** (7.07 vs ~11.00 N/mm² central and tail) | longitudinal strongest (12.05 N/mm²) |
| **Croaker** | — | transverse strongest at head, longitudinal at tail |

Tilapia tear strength: **tail 49.65 N/mm vs head and central ~61.49 N/mm** ✓.

**So the "weak tail" intuition is species-specific, not a fish rule.** Tilapia tears easiest
at the tail *and* head; pirarucu is weakest at the head; croaker is strongest at the head
transversely. A pattern written for one species does not transfer.

Salmon: thin, light, roughly **60–65 cm long × 10–14 cm wide** (~0.8 sq ft) ✓ — a narrow
strip, so parts are long and thin by necessity.

## The full fish roster — surface types and what each is for

The leather-dictionary reference classifies fish leather by **surface**, which is the right
axis, because fish species differ far more in surface than in structure ✓.

| Surface type | Species it covers | Pattern consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale structure** | perch, dolphinfish, carp, **cod**, **salmon**, pacu, **pirarucu/arapaima**, parrotfish, **tilapia**, plaice | the scale pattern is the product. Directional; keep the row alignment along a strap |
| **Relatively smooth** | some sharks, **Atlantic stingray**, **catfish** | reads as a plain leather; the species becomes invisible |
| **Central scar** | **eel**, salmon, pacu, plaice | a **ribbed central strip** — a feature to design around, not to hide |
| **Scaly only under the microscope** | some sharks | normal leather feel |
| **Hard pearl studs, sometimes sanded flat** | some stingrays, **kitefin shark** | armour; see the stingray section above |
| **Visible pores** | **moray eel**, **sea wolf / wolffish** | the pores are the texture — no scales at all on wolffish ✓ |
| **Driftwood-like** | **sturgeon** | scute rows run along the body; strongly directional |

### The species worth knowing individually

| Fish | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Salmon** | the volume leader (~34 % of the fish-leather market ~) | thin, 0.5–1.0 mm; **~60–65 × 10–14 cm** ✓; veg or chrome-free tanned usually ✓ |
| **Wolffish / sea wolf** | **no scales — only spots** ✓ | bold pattern, highest premium per m² ~; from Icelandic demersal fisheries ✓ |
| **Cod** | plain, durable | favoured in artisan footwear ~; ~12 % of market ~ |
| **Perch / ocean perch** | small scales | **~1.0 sq ft per skin** ✓ — 50–60 cm long but only 14–20 cm wide |
| **Eel** | **no scales**; smooth and robust ✓ | long thin strip with a ribbed centre ✓; traditionally European |
| **Sturgeon** | driftwood surface ✓ | scute rows; historically prized (Nile perch and sturgeon were among the earliest fish leathers ✓) |
| **Tilapia** | cheap, large-ish scales | region effects documented — see the table above |
| **Pirarucu / arapaima** | very large scales | head region weakest ✓ |
| **Haddock** | niche | ~7 % of market ~ |
| **Catfish** | smooth surface ✓ | — |
| **Moray eel** | visible pores ✓ | — |
| **Boxfish, puffer / ball fish** | novelty | small, oddly shaped |

### What fish leather is like to work, in the trade's own words

Five consistent complaints ~:

1. **Difficult to make a good natural burnished edge**
2. A learning curve
3. Hard to find a supplier
4. **The flesh side can peel back when applying glue or removing stickers**
5. **Small hide to work with**

That fourth point is an anatomy issue: the **flesh split is weak**, so anything relying on an
adhesive bond to the back of a fish leather is relying on the weakest layer. Line it, or test
the glue on scrap.

### Fibre direction — the structural claim worth keeping

**Fish skin fibre runs crosswise rather than parallel to the body axis, as in cowhide** ✓, and
this is why fish leather is strong for its thickness. Compare the bovine L4 direction axis:
the reason a cow strap is cut along the backbone is that the fibres run that way. On a fish you
are **cutting across the fibre** whenever you cut along the body — which is why the scales and
the strength run in the *other* direction, and why the three-species study found
**longitudinal > diagonal** for some species and **transverse > longitudinal** for others.

## What to specify

```
MATERIAL  Stingray (L0) · dorsal wing (L1) · denticles retained and polished (L3.5),
          crown cluster present · flat panel only (L4: n/a)
MATERIAL  Shark (L0) · denticles removed (L3.5) · 1.0 mm · behaves as normal leather
MATERIAL  Salmon (L0) · mid-body (L1) · scales retained · ~0.8 sq ft per skin
```

## Honest limits

- **Do not quote durability multiples.** Claims that stingray is "25 times more durable than
  cowhide" ~ or that ostrich leg is "3× cowhide tensile" ✓~ are vendor figures with no test
  method attached. Use the mechanism (enameloid caps, Mohs ~5, fused beads) and let the
  number go.
- **Fish fibre direction** — "crosswise rather than parallel" ✓ — is a genuine structural
  statement, but it is stated at the level of the whole skin. The three-species study above
  shows it resolves into species-specific direction effects.
- **CITES/fisheries rules differ** by species and region. Not anatomy.
