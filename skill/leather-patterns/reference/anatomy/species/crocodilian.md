# Crocodilian — crocodile, alligator, caiman

The hide is graded, measured and priced **almost entirely on the belly**, and the rest of the
animal is either decoration or a different product. This is the clearest case in the trade of
a skin where the commercial value is concentrated in **two of the five L1 regions**.

---

## The regions

| Region | What it is | Value |
|---|---|---|
| **Belly** | the ventral surface; regular, rectangular "brick" or diamond scales | **the most valuable part**; softest, most uniform, largest usable area |
| **Flank / side** | scales transition from rectangular to circular | intermediate — softer than the back, less uniform than the belly |
| **Back / hornback** | large raised scales over bony plates; the dorsal ridge | tough but **hard**; decorative, statement pieces; heavier. Historically rarely used for high-end bags ✓~ |
| **Tail** | wide, longer scales, irregular | usable, often lower grade; the tail is **not** included in grading |
| **Head** | small, bony, marked | **not** included in grading |
| **Legs / feet** | small | small parts, claws sold separately |

## The single most important property: calcium

**Genuine crocodile and alligator *belly* leather has minimal calcium, which is why it bends
smoothly without cracking. Caiman skin is embedded with osteoderms — hydroxyapatite plates —
which make it stiff and brittle along fold lines** ✓.

So the hard-part level (`../appendages.md` L3.5) is not an appendix here; on caiman it is
the whole story. It also explains two side-effects:

- **Caiman creases visibly between the scales**, sometimes showing white stress lines where
  the calcium fractures ✓
- **Caiman dyes unevenly**, because the calcium plates take dye differently from the
  surrounding tissue ✓

Test it by hand: flex the piece across its width. A genuine belly **flexes in one smooth
motion**; caiman **creases between the scales** ✓.

## Grading — the numbers to know

Grading considers **the midsection only: belly plus flank.** "The condition of the head and
tail are not typically considered in the grading" ✓.

| Grade | American alligator (5-tier) ✓ | American crocodile (4-tier) ✓ | Approx. value ~ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | no defects in the belly | no defects in the belly | 100 % |
| 2 | one cluster of defects on the outer edge of belly or flank | a solitary defect / small cluster at the outer edge of belly | ~75 % |
| 3 | one cluster of defects **in the centre belly** | defect near the centre of the belly | ~50 % |
| 4 | clusters of defects in **two different quadrants** of the midsection | defects in more than one quadrant | lowest |
| 5 | worse than 4 | — | — |

**Grade is not a quality statement about the material — it is a map of where the scars are.**
A Grade 3 skin is the same leather as a Grade 1 with a defect in the middle. If your parts
are small enough to cut around a centre-belly scar, a Grade 3 skin is the rational buy. That
is a **nesting** decision, and this is one of the few exotics where it is easy to exploit
deliberately. One supplier's own guidance makes exactly this split: **Grade 3 or better for
footwear, wallets and garments; Grade 5 or better for belts and small goods** ✓.

## Measurement — by belly width, not length

Skins are measured **across the widest point of the belly**, excluding hard bone ✓.

| American alligator, wild ✓ | 30–100 cm, average ~50 cm |
|---|---|
| American alligator, farm ✓ | mostly 20–44 cm, average ~30 cm |
| American crocodile ✓ | 35–75 cm, majority 50–69 cm |

PanAm's published size table is worth carrying because it shows how **every other dimension
scales with the quoted belly width** — for a 50/54 cm skin: head 35 × 32 cm, belly 36 × 47 cm,
tail 90 cm long × 33 cm at the top, average thickness **1.5 mm** ✓. Thickness across the
whole range runs **0.8 mm (20/24 cm) → 1.8 mm (60/64 cm)** ✓.

> **This is the useful part for a pattern maker.** Because the quoted "size" is a width, you
> can predict the belly panel dimensions before buying. A 30/34 cm skin gives roughly a
> **35 × 32 cm** belly ✓ — which is what actually decides whether your panel fits.

## Scale rows — a countable structure

American crocodile: **25–34 rows of belly scales, 14–16 scales per row** ✓.

For pattern work this matters more than it sounds: the **pitch of the scales is a fixed
physical grid**. If a stitch line crosses the belly, it will either land in the scale
valleys (tidy) or run across the crowns (messy), and the row count tells you how many
crowns you have to play with.

## Species, as far as it affects the material

| Species | Traded as | Material signature |
|---|---|---|
| **Crocodylus porosus** (saltwater) | Porosus | finest, most uniform rectangular scales; distinct pores in the centre of each scale; softest; top grade ✓~ |
| **Crocodylus niloticus** (Nile) | Niloticus | rectangular scale pattern over a wide area; pores present; larger skins, dyes well ✓~ |
| **Alligator mississippiensis** | Alligator | **no pores**; medium-to-large scales, smooth appearance with three small dashes at the bottom of each scale; larger flank and narrower belly than most crocodiles; wider tail ✓ |

The **pore** distinction is a real anatomical discriminator: **integumentary sensory organs
(ISOs)** sit near the centre of each scale. True crocodiles (genus *Crocodylus*) have them
across the whole body; **alligators and caimans only on the head and jaw** ✓.

## Pattern consequences

| Property | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Belly is flat, regular, low-calcium | **can be used for structural parts and folded edges** — unusual among exotics |
| Scales are a rigid grid | A fold through the belly will hinge at the scale boundary, not where you drew it. Place the flex line **between** two scale rows |
| Hornback is raised and bony | adds bulk and weight; will not sit flat in a closed stack; never a lining-side piece |
| Directional | Scale size changes head→tail; a strap cut along the body has a **graduating** texture, one cut across it is uniform |
| Small usable belly area | Budget **2–4 belly skins for a single medium handbag** ~ — plan the whole object around the belly panel first |

## What to specify

```
MATERIAL  Alligator mississippiensis, farmed (L0) · belly, midsection (L1/L2) ·
          scales intact, low calcium (L3.5) · grade 3 or better · belly 40/44 cm →
          belly panel ~36 x 42 cm, 1.3 mm · CITES-tagged
```

## Honest limits

- **Grading is a vendor/industry convention, not an ISO standard.** PanAm's 5-tier system for
  alligator and 4-tier for crocodile are documented ✓ but not universal; another source
  describes a flat 4-grade system where **each grade drop cuts wholesale value by ~25 %** ✓.
  Do not present a grade number as if it were portable between sellers.
- **Value percentages (~100 / 75 / 50 %)** come from one trade article ✓, not a price index.
- **Species→softness rankings** come from retail explainers ~. The pore/ISO distinction is
  the anatomical part and is solid; "softest" is a market opinion.
- **Legality is not anatomy.** Crocodilians are CITES-listed and the rules differ by species
  and by whether the skin is wild or captive-bred. Check it; do not infer it from a hide.
