# Poultry — ostrich, emu, rhea, chicken, turkey, duck, goose

Ostrich has its own file (`ostrich.md`) because one bird yields four materials. This file
covers **the rest of the birds**, and the structural question they all pose: what does an
avian skin do differently from a mammalian one?

---

## Two avian facts that change everything

**1. Feathers are follicles — and on most birds they are in tracts.**

A bird's skin carries feathers in organised **feather tracts**, not scattered uniformly. On
ostrich the consequence is the **crown** — a dense quill field covering about a third of the
skin. On **emu** the follicles are **scattered uniformly across the hide**, producing a small,
even "**feather pin**" texture rather than a crown ✓~. So:

| | ostrich | emu |
|---|---|---|
| follicle arrangement | **concentrated** — the crown, a diamond, ⅓ of the skin | **uniform** across the hide |
| the pattern | large raised quill bumps, graded by quarters | small even feather-pin texture |
| layout consequence | you cannot get quill on every part | **you can cut anywhere** and keep the texture |

That is the whole difference between the two bird leathers, and it is a difference of
**distribution**, not of quality.

**2. Bird skins are thin, and the legs are where the hard parts are.**

Avian skin is generally thinner than mammalian hide of the same animal size, and the hard
keratinised material is concentrated on the **legs and feet** rather than spread over the body.
Which is why the legs are a separate product across the whole family — ostrich leg, chicken
leg, and "feet" as a novelty item.

---

## Emu — *Dromaius novaehollandiae*

| Property | Value | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Surface | **distinctive grain from feathers: small follicle marks ("feather pins") scattered uniformly** | ✓~ |
| Hand | **exceptionally soft and lightweight; a lightly quilled, fine pebbled texture**; among the softest natural leathers | ~ |
| Dyeing | **accepts a wide range of colours readily** | ~ |
| Durability | resists drying, cracking and breaking; soft but surprisingly durable | ~ |
| Yield | **6–8 sq ft per fully grown bird** | ~ |
| Use | belts, wallets, bags, lightweight garments, accessories | ~ |
| Availability | scarce and expensive; often described as second only to crocodile in price | ~ marketing, discount |

**Pattern note:** emu is the **anti-ostrich**. Where ostrich forces you to plan around the
crown, emu's uniform pin texture means the layout is free — the constraint becomes **area and
softness**, not placement. A soft, thin, uniformly-textured hide is a lining-and-small-goods
material, and it is weak enough that it should be lined in anything load-bearing.

---

## Rhea

~ Niche. Treated in the trade as emu-like: thin, soft, small follicle texture. There is very
little reliable published material. **Say so rather than inventing a spec.**

---

## Chicken — sold as "poulard" and as chicken-leg

This is the bird that proves **how small a leather workshop can go**.

| Property | Value | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Source | **the skin of the legs and feet in practice**; carcass skin in principle | ✓~ |
| Thickness | thin — "as thick as emu leather" | ~ |
| Strength | thin, **low tensile strength, little resistance to wear**; also described as *superior to calf and sheepskin* for the leg-skin product | **✗ these two claims directly contradict** |
| Size | **tiny** — "in order to use one leather strap, you'll need at least 2 hen hides" | ~ |
| Surface | **small scales like ostrich leg, more elastic, with a thick grain pattern**; resembles ostrich shin and reptile | ✓~ |
| Trade name | **poulard** | ✓~ |

**Do not quote a strength claim for chicken leather.** Two sources in the same search gave
opposite verdicts — "less durable and low tensile strength, unsuitable for sturdy boots" and
"superior to many classic calf and sheepskin types." The reconcilable reading is that they are
describing different products: **carcass skin is weak; leg skin with its keratinised scales is
tough.** State the substrate, not the species.

**Pattern note:** chicken leg is the cheap route to an ostrich-leg-like scale texture, at the
cost of joinery — several hens per strap. Design the joins as a feature or choose another
material.

---

## Turkey, duck, goose

~ All are named in the exotic-leather literature and none has a body of reliable material
data. **Goose** is often cited as a mid-size bird skin comparable to turkey; **duck** skins
appear in novelty goods. Treat the whole group as: thin, small, soft, patterned by follicle
marks, and **unproven for anything structural**.

---

## What applies to every bird skin

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Line it for anything load-bearing | Use it as the outer of a hinge or strap under tension |
| Expect softness as the headline property | Expect abrasion resistance from the body skin |
| Use the legs for edges, facings, decorative termini | Use a claw or a nail end anywhere that must fold |
| Check follicle **distribution** before planning layout | Assume ostrich-style crown behaviour on other birds |
| Ask for the substrate: body skin or leg skin | Accept "chicken leather" or "emu leather" as a specification |

## What to specify

```
MATERIAL  Emu (L0) · body, uniform feather-pin texture (L3.5) · 0.6-0.8 mm ·
          lined, small goods only
MATERIAL  Chicken (L0) · leg skin (L1/L2) · keratinised scales intact (L3.5) ·
          part must fit within ~15 cm, joins planned
```

## Honest limits

- **Emu and rhea specs come from supplier and enthusiast sources**, not from measurement.
- **Chicken strength claims are contradictory** and are recorded as a conflict, not resolved.
- **No regional anatomy exists** for any poultry skin except ostrich. Bird skins are small
  enough that the region question rarely arises — which is itself the answer: on a 6 sq ft emu
  hide there is no "bend" to cut from.
