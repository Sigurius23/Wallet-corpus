# L3.5 — hard parts: the axis that exotic leather adds

The bovine framework in `taxonomy.md` has four levels: animal, region, cut, layer, plus
direction. That covers a cow. **It does not cover an ostrich, a crocodile or a stingray**,
because those animals have **keratinised or calcified appendages embedded in or replacing
the skin** — and the appendages, not the skin, are usually what you are buying.

This level sits between the layer (L3) and the direction (L4) axes, so call it **L3.5**.

---

## Why this is not a detail

A normal leather is a **soft, continuous, fibrous sheet**. Every rule in `../durability.md`
assumes that: stress is spread, strain is continuous, a crack must be initiated in
homogeneous material.

A hide with hard parts is a **composite of brittle plates in a soft matrix**. That changes
the failure mode completely:

| | soft leather | leather with hard parts |
|---|---|---|
| Where does it fail? | anywhere the strain concentrates | **at the boundary between plate and matrix** |
| Crack path | must initiate in fibre | runs **between** the plates, so it needs almost no initiation energy |
| Flexing | strain spreads over the fold | plates **hinge against each other**; the fold localises to one inter-plate gap |
| Skiving / splitting | reduces thickness evenly | **cut through a composite** — removes the hard part on one face and leaves a different material |
| Edge finishing | burnish or paint | plates chip; the edge is a row of hard corners |
| The `ε = t/(2R+t)` screen | screening heuristic | **meaningless** — the strain in a plate at a hinge is not the bulk fold strain |
| Moisture | softens | the soft matrix softens, **the plates do not** — so drying increases the mismatch |

**The one sentence:** *the harder the surface, the more the failure is an interface problem
and the less it is a bulk-material problem.*

## The catalogue

Grouped by what the hard part actually is. This is the taxonomy the trade never writes down.

### 1. Dermal bone — osteoderms

| Animal | What | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Crocodile, alligator, caiman** | bony plates (osteoderms) under the dorsal scales, the "hornback" | **caiman is stiff and brittle along fold lines**; genuine crocodile and alligator *belly* has minimal calcium, which is why belly bends smoothly ✓ |

This is the single most useful hard-part fact in the trade: **the calcium content of the
ventral skin is what makes crocodilian belly leather work and caiman leather not work.**
Caiman osteoderms also absorb dye differently from surrounding tissue, so it dyes unevenly ✓.
The **calcium bend test** — flex the piece across its width; a genuine belly flexes in one
smooth motion, caiman creases visible between the scales and can show white stress lines
where the calcium fractures ✓ — is a mechanical test of this exact property.

### 2. Keratinised scales and scutes

| Animal | What | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Snake** | scales are **folds of the skin itself**, continuous with the tissue between them, made of α-keratin under β-keratin ✓ — not separate plates sitting on top | so they cannot simply be removed; and the failure mode is **lifting/peeling at the free edge**, documented on an upholstered python seat from 1930 and on modern wallets ✓ |
| **Ostrich leg** | large semi-rectangular **scutes** in a central column down the **front**; small fine scales on the sides and back ✓ | the same skin is rigid down the front and soft at the back |
| **Crocodilian** | non-overlapping tile scales; belly rows are regular and countable | gives the species a **measurable** structure: e.g. American crocodile belly 25–34 rows of 14–16 scales ✓ |
| **Lizard** | fine, uniform, tightly packed pebble scales, the "ring mark" pattern | small skins, regular pattern |
| **Pangolin / armadillo** | true keratin plates, not leather in the normal sense | niche; state that the material is a plate assembly, not a hide |

### 3. Dermal denticles — the fish armour

| Animal | What | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Stingray** | hard calcified beads (**denticles**): an enameloid cap over a dentin core with a pulp cavity; fluorapatite, not hydroxyapatite ✓. **They cannot grow once formed** — the animal adds new ones *between* existing ones, so bead size varies by body region ✓ | the surface is a mosaic of fixed-size hard islands. **Nothing peels or lifts** because the beads are fused to the hide ✓ — the opposite of snake. But **the panel wants to stay flat** ✓: low flexibility, so it is an accessory/panel/inlay material, never a gusset |
| **Shark** | much smaller, sharper denticles in regular rows toward the tail; **usually ground off** in tanning, leaving a fine pebbled grain ✓ | with denticles removed, shark behaves like ordinary leather (flexes, conditions, softens with age) and hides are large (~30 × 20 in) ✓ |

**Same word, opposite material.** Both stingray and shark were called *shagreen* for
centuries ✓. One keeps its armour, the other has it sanded away. A spec must say which.

### 4. Follicle craters — the ostrich quill

| Animal | What | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Ostrich body** | each quill is an **empty feather follicle** with a raised keratinised collar around a cavity — a "fat pocket" ✓ | not a hard plate: a **soft hollow dome**. So ostrich creases and softens like calf rather than cracking like a scaled hide ✓ |

The quill is the counter-example that stops L3.5 becoming "hard parts are stiff". A quill
bump is raised but **compliant**. Two consequences worth knowing:

- **Skiving is harder than it looks.** The bumps sit at different heights, so a flat skive
  cuts through the raised ones ✓. Ostrich needs a more careful skive than calf.
- **The pattern wears flat where it flexes.** Along a shoe vamp the quills compress and
  polish until a smooth band forms across the flex point ✓. This is a *localised*
  flattening, not general wear — so place the flex line deliberately.

### 5. Nails, claws and hooves

The detail that makes the ostrich the best example in this whole folder — and the one the
brief specifically asked about.

The ostrich foot is **didactyl: two toes.** The **first (inner) toe is enormous** with a
wide, nearly hoof-like nail; the **second is much shorter, flattened, and has no claw at
all** ✓. The foot is described as functionally closer to an ungulate's than to a typical
bird's ✓. On the sole, scales are **very small, highly keratinised, and form dense pads** ✓;
the nail is short, wide, blunt and **feels nearly like a hoof** ✓.

Consequences for anyone buying "ostrich leg with the claw attached" (which is how it is
sold ~):

| Feature | Pattern consequence |
|---|---|
| Toe pads: dense small keratinised scales | **abrasion-resistant** — good for a wear edge, bad for anything needing to flex |
| Hoof-like nail | a **rigid inclusion** at one end of the skin — you cannot fold, skive or stitch near it. It is a decorative terminus, not a workable region |
| Front of leg: large scutes; back of leg: soft loose skin with big folds ✓ | the leg skin is **its own front/back map**. The back was built to fold; the front was built not to |
| Two toes, so two claw ends | each leg skin has **two** rigid tips, not one — check which end the seller cut |

**This is the general rule for feet and lower legs on any species:** the further down the
leg you go, the more the skin stops being skin and becomes **wear surface**. It gets denser,
more keratinised, less vascular, and the fibre weave angle flattens. It is excellent at
resisting abrasion and terrible at deforming. Use it for edges, facings, reinforcements and
decorative termini — **never** for a hinge or a part that must stretch.

That is also why **ostrich leg is the toughest of the three ostrich cuts** and **holds the
sharpest burnished edge** ✓ — its fibre runs tighter.

## How to specify a hard-part material

Add a level to the spec line:

```
MATERIAL  Crocodilian (L0, Crocodylus porosus) · belly (L2), midsection (L1) ·
          scales intact, no osteoderms (L3.5: none) · 1.3 mm · grade 2 · CITES-tagged
MATERIAL  Stingray (L0) · dorsal wing (L1) · denticles retained and polished (L3.5) ·
          crown/eye cluster present · panel only, flat-work (L4: n/a)
MATERIAL  Ostrich (L0) · leg skin, front scute column + back folds (L1) · claw removed (L3.5) ·
          1.2-1.6 mm
```

Three rules that follow:

1. **State whether the hard part is retained or removed.** Retained vs ground-off is the
   difference between stingray and shark, and between hornback and belly.
2. **State which direction the hard parts run.** Scutes and denticle rows are directional;
   a strap cut across them will hinge at the wrong pitch.
3. **Never apply the fold strain screen.** `ε = t/(2R+t)` describes a continuous sheet.
   For a plated material, ask instead: *does the fold line fall between two plates?* If it
   falls through a plate, the plate is what fails.

## What this level cannot tell you

- **Whether a specific skin is legal to trade.** That is CITES, not anatomy. Species-level
  listing status is a separate check, and it changes.
- **How thick a plate is on your particular skin.** Osteoderm and denticle size vary with
  age, sex and body region — stingray tubercles are larger and denser in females ~.
- **Whether the pattern is real.** Embossed imitations are outside this taxonomy entirely.
  The reliable discriminators are anatomical: a genuine ostrich quill has an **open pore at
  its apex** that a stamp cannot reproduce ✓; genuine crocodile transition scales **change
  gradually** across the body whereas a stamp repeats ✓; alligator has an **umbilical scar**
  (elongated star-shaped cluster of small irregular belly scales) that is hard to fake ✓.
